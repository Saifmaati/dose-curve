How doses of different routes are modelled

Every dose goes into the same body (one compartment, or the central one of two), with one volume and one clearance whatever the route. Oral doses are absorbed at the rate kₐ and only the fraction F reaches the blood; IV doses count in full. An infusion delivers its amount at a constant rate (amount ÷ duration) from start to stop, with no delay in the line. Everything adds up: overlapping infusions add their rates, and a bolus given during an infusion adds on top.

Can’t add another dose: the schedule already reaches the 168-hour limit. Duplicate still adds a dose at the same time.

Patient

Weight for Cockcroft–Gault

Which weight?

Cockcroft and Gault derived their equation with actual body weight. For patients well above their ideal weight, many references use ideal body weight (IBW, Devine: 50 kg for men or 45.5 kg for women, plus 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet), or an adjusted weight, IBW + 0.4 × (actual − IBW). Some use actual weight when it is below IBW. Conventions differ between references and institutions, so the choice is left to you here.

Individualize from levels (Bayesian)

Enter measured levels, each as hours after the start of a dose. The estimate weighs them against this patient model's prediction (the prior), each by how uncertain it is.

Teaching assumptions: the prior's CVs are the Population settings' (clearance 30%, volume 20% unless changed there); each level's error SD is √((10% of the level)² + (MEC / 10)²); one compartment, first-order elimination. Method: Sheiner et al., Clin Pharmacol Ther 1979. Educational model, not for clinical dosing.

Renal maturation follows Rhodin et al. (Pediatr Nephrol 2009), from measured filtration rates between 22 weeks' postmenstrual age and adulthood. The drug's non-renal clearance is scaled by size alone: how it matures depends on the enzymes involved, which this model doesn't include. Educational model, not for clinical dosing.

Pharmacodynamics

How concentration becomes effect

Indirect responses (Dayneka, Garg and Jusko, 1993): the drug inhibits or stimulates the production (kin) or the loss (kout) of a response R, such as a protein the body makes and clears, with a turnover half-life ln 2 / kout. R is shown as a percentage of its baseline, kin / kout = 100%. With f = Cⁿ / (EC50ⁿ + Cⁿ): type 1, dR/dt = kin·(1 − Imax·f) − kout·R; type 2, kin − kout·(1 − Imax·f)·R; type 3, kin·(1 + Smax·f) − kout·R; type 4, kin − kout·(1 + Smax·f)·R. The response lags the level, and a slow turnover makes the lag longer. With Imax = 1 and n = 1 these are the paper's equations. The target effect applies to direct effects only.

Effect follows the sigmoid Emax model, E = E₀ + Emax·Cⁿ / (EC50ⁿ + Cⁿ), as a percentage of the largest possible response. EC50 is the concentration that gives half of Emax (potency), Emax the ceiling (efficacy) and n how steeply the effect turns on around EC50. The effect follows plasma concentration instantly and fully reversibly. This simplified direct-effect model does not model delayed effects, tolerance, active metabolites, indirect responses, or any individual patient's outcome. The curve is an empirical description, not a mechanism.

Therapeutic window

Antimicrobial PK/PD

How the indices are read

fT>MIC is the share of time the unbound level (fu × the total level, drawn dotted) stays above the MIC, with fu taken as constant. Cmax/MIC and AUC24/MIC use the total level, as the 2020 vancomycin guideline's AUC24/MIC does; fu times each gives the unbound version. A repeated regimen is read at steady state over one dosing interval (a loading or missed dose doesn't change it), with AUC24 = the AUC over one interval × 24 / τ. A single dose or a custom schedule is read over the time window, with the AUC over the first 24 hours. AUC24/MIC has units of hours (mg·h/L ÷ mg/L) and is usually quoted without one.

The MIC is the organism's, measured in the laboratory; 0 turns these readouts off. Educational model, not for clinical dosing.

    Understand the modelTap a concept
    FBioavailability›
    The fraction of an oral dose that survives absorption and first-pass metabolism to reach the bloodstream. IV routes bypass this entirely, so F = 1. Halving F halves every concentration on the curve.
    kₐAbsorption rate›
    How fast drug moves from the gut into plasma. High kₐ → sharp early peak; low kₐ (extended-release) → flatter, later peak with the same total exposure (AUC). Try sliding it and watch Tmax move.
    t½Half-life & kₑ›
    Time for concentration to fall by half. Linked to the elimination constant by kₑ = ln2 / t½. On the LOG scale, elimination becomes a straight line — its slope is −kₑ. That's the classic way pharmacologists read half-life off a plot.
    VVolume of distribution›
    The apparent volume the dose dissolves into: C = amount / V. Drugs that hide in tissue have huge V and low plasma levels. V scales with body weight — move the patient weight slider and watch the whole curve rescale.
    CLClearance›
    The volume of plasma cleared of drug per hour: CL = kₑ·V. Reduced organ function lowers CL, stretching the half-life and raising accumulation — simulate it with the organ function slider.
    AUCExposure›
    Area under the curve — total drug exposure. For first-order kinetics AUC = F·D / CL, independent of absorption speed. That's why an ER formulation changes the shape but not the area.
    SSSteady state›
    With repeated dosing, drug accumulates until input = elimination. ~90% of steady state arrives after 3.3 half-lives regardless of dose or interval. A loading dose jumps you there immediately — toggle it and compare.
    TWTherapeutic window›
    The band between minimum effective (MEC) and toxic (MTC) concentration. Good regimens maximize time in window — tracked live in the readouts. Narrow-window drugs like theophylline make this a genuine engineering problem.
    LDLoading dose›
    Getting to a target and staying there are different jobs. Conceptually, LD ≈ C_target·V / F: filling the volume sets the load. The maintenance rate ≈ C_target·CL / F: clearance sets what it takes to stay. A load changes how fast you arrive, not where you settle.
    ✕Missed dose›
    Skipping a dose leaves a gap in the input while elimination carries on, so the next trough dips well below the usual one. Recovery back to the regular pattern takes roughly as long as reaching steady state in the first place, which is a few half-lives. Set one with the missed dose slider.
